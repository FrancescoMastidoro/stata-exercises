## Background
The `nlsw88` dataset contains 1988 survey data on American women aged 34–46, covering wages, employment, education, and demographics from the National Longitudinal Survey. In quantitative labor economics research, visualization is a critical first step: plots reveal distributional features, group differences, and variable relationships that summary statistics alone can obscure.

## Your tasks

1. **Plot the wage distribution** — Load the dataset and draw a histogram of `wage`. Add a title using `title()` and label the x-axis with `xtitle()`. Then redraw the histogram using the `normal` option to overlay a normal distribution curve — does the wage distribution look approximately normal, or does it appear skewed?

2. **Compare wage distributions across race groups** — Use `histogram wage` with the `by(race)` option to produce separate histogram panels for each racial group. Note how the `by()` option splits the plot — do the distributions look similar across groups?

3. **Scatter plot of wage against total experience** — Use `twoway scatter` to plot `wage` on the y-axis against `ttl_exp` on the x-axis. Add a linear fit line by combining `scatter` and `lfit` within a single `twoway` command, separating them with `||`. Add appropriate axis titles and a legend.

4. **Bar chart of mean wage by occupation** — Use `graph bar wage, over(occupation)` to display mean wages across occupational categories. Then add the option `asyvars` and observe how the appearance changes. Note: `occupation` has 9 missing values — Stata will silently exclude these from the bar chart by default.

5. *(Bonus)* **Twoway plot combining groups** — Use `twoway scatter wage ttl_exp, by(collgrad)` to visualize whether the relationship between experience and wages differs between college graduates and non-graduates. Then try adding a `lfit` line within each panel by including both `scatter` and `lfit` inside the `by()` framework. Does the slope of the fit line appear steeper for college graduates?
