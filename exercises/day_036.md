## Background
The Mroz (1987) dataset is a classic in labor economics, drawn from the PSID and containing detailed information on wives' and husbands' labor market outcomes, family structure, and earnings. Mastering t-tests, regression, and marginal effects on this dataset will give you a solid foundation for evaluating wage gaps, participation decisions, and returns to education.

## Your tasks

1. **Descriptive comparison by labor force status** — Tabulate the `lfp` variable to see how many wives are and are not in the labor force. Then compute the mean and standard deviation of `wifeage` and `wedyrs` separately for women who participate (`lfp == 1`) and those who do not (`lfp == 0`) using `summarize` with `if`.

2. **Two-sample t-test on wife's education** — Test whether the mean number of years of education (`wedyrs`) differs significantly between wives who participate in the labor force and those who do not. Use `ttest` with the `by()` option and interpret the direction and significance of the difference at the 5% level.

3. **Simple OLS regression of wages on education** — Restricting the sample to wives who are in the labor force (`lfp == 1`), regress `wwage76` (wife's hourly wage in 1976) on `wedyrs`. Report the estimated coefficient on education and explain what it implies economically.

4. **Multiple regression with controls** — Extend the model from Task 3 by adding `wexper`, `kl6` (kids under 6), and `smsa` as additional regressors. Compare the coefficient on `wedyrs` before and after adding controls — does it change substantially? Also note whether `smsa` is statistically significant at the 10% level.

5. *(Bonus)* **Marginal effect of urban residence** — After running the multiple regression from Task 4, use `margins, dydx(smsa)` to compute the average marginal effect of living in an SMSA on the wife's wage. Then re-run the regression replacing `smsa` with `i.smsa` and use `margins i.smsa` to compute predicted wages in each group. Explain why the two approaches give numerically identical point estimates in a linear OLS model.

## Hints

- **Hint 1 (Task 2)**: The syntax for a two-sample t-test using a binary grouping variable is `ttest varname, by(groupvar)`. Check whether Stata reports a one-sided or two-sided p-value by default, and think about which one is appropriate for a non-directional hypothesis.

- **Hint 2 (Task 3)**: Remember that `wwage76` records a wage for all 753 observations in this dataset (no missing values), but you should still restrict to `lfp == 1` to model wages for labour-force participants only — non-participants have an unobserved wage. Use `regress wwage76 wedyrs if lfp == 1`.

- **Hint 3 (Task 4)**: After running the extended regression, look at the column labelled `P>|t|` for the `smsa` row. A coefficient is significant at 10% if that value is below 0.10. To compare coefficients across specifications, write down the point estimate from Task 3 and the new one side by side.

- **Hint 4 (Task 5 — Bonus)**: In a linear regression, `margins, dydx(x)` simply returns the OLS coefficient on `x`. The real insight from `margins i.smsa` is that it gives you predicted wages at each value of `smsa`, holding all other variables at their observed values — a useful habit to build even when the result is algebraically obvious in OLS.
