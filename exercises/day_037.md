## Background
The NLSY 1980 dataset contains 935 observations on young workers, including wages, education, cognitive ability, and labor market characteristics. Mastering t-tests, regression, and marginal effects is essential for labor economics research, where you need to move from simple comparisons to controlled estimates and interpretable quantities.

## Your tasks

1. **Descriptive comparison** — Compute the mean monthly wage (`wage`) separately for workers who attended college (`college == 1`) and those who did not (`college == 0`). Then run a two-sample t-test to determine whether the difference in means is statistically significant at the 5% level. Report the t-statistic and your conclusion.

2. **Baseline OLS regression** — Regress the log wage (`lwage`) on `educ`, `exper`, `tenure`, and `black`. Interpret the coefficient on `educ`: what does a one-year increase in education predict for log wages, holding other variables constant?

3. **Extend the model** — Add `married`, `south`, and `urban` to the regression from Task 2. Do the coefficients on `educ` and `black` change noticeably? Comment on what this might suggest about omitted variable bias in the baseline model.

4. **Margins after regression** — Using the extended model from Task 3, use `margins` to compute the average predicted log wage for workers who are college graduates (`college == 1`) versus those who are not (`college == 0`). First add `college` to your regression, then run `margins, at(college=(0 1))`. How large is the predicted gap?

5. *(Bonus)* **Interaction and marginal effects** — Re-run the extended model from Task 4 but add an interaction term between `college` and `black`. Use `margins black, dydx(college)` to estimate the marginal effect of college attendance on log wages separately for Black and non-Black workers. Are the returns to college equal across groups? Test formally using `margins black, dydx(college)` and inspecting the confidence intervals.

## Hints

<summary>Click to reveal hints (try first!)</summary>

- **Hint 1 (Task 1)**: The t-test command for comparing means across two groups defined by a binary variable uses the syntax `ttest varname, by(groupvar)` — look at which row gives you the two-sided p-value.
- **Hint 2 (Task 2)**: Remember that `lwage` is already the natural log of `wage`, so OLS coefficients on continuous regressors have a semi-elasticity interpretation (multiply by 100 for a percentage effect).
- **Hint 3 (Task 3)**: To compare coefficients across models, consider storing results with `estimates store` after each regression — this lets you use `estimates table` for a side-by-side view.
- **Hint 4 (Task 4)**: `margins` works on the most recently estimated model. Make sure `college` is included in the regression *before* calling `margins, at(college=(0 1))`. The `at()` option fixes the named variable at specified values and averages over all other covariates.
- **Hint 5 (Bonus)**: To include an interaction in Stata, use `c.college#i.black` or simply create a product variable. After estimation, `margins black, dydx(college)` computes the derivative (or discrete difference) of the outcome with respect to `college` at each level of `black`.
