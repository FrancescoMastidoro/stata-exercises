## Background
The Mroz (1987) dataset is a classic in labor economics, containing information on 753 married women from the PSID, including their labor force participation, wages, education, and family structure. Mastering t-tests, regression, and marginal effects in this context will give you a core toolkit for answering causal questions about wages and participation in empirical work.

## Your tasks

1. **Descriptive comparison** — Using `ttest`, test whether wives' hourly wages in 1975 (`wwage75`) differ significantly between women who live in an SMSA (`smsa == 1`) and those who do not (`smsa == 0`). Report the t-statistic and whether you can reject the null at the 5% level. Remember that `wwage75` ranges from 0 to 25 and has no missing values.

2. **Simple OLS regression** — Regress the wife's 1975 hourly wage (`wwage75`) on her years of education (`wedyrs`) using `regress`. Interpret the coefficient on `wedyrs`: what does a one-year increase in education predict for the hourly wage?

3. **Multiple regression** — Extend the regression from Task 2 by adding `wexper` (work experience), `kl6` (number of children under 6), and `smsa` as additional controls. Comment on how the coefficient on `wedyrs` changes compared to Task 2, and whether the sign on `kl6` matches your economic intuition.

4. **Marginal effects after regression** — After running the multiple regression from Task 3, use `margins` to compute the average marginal effect of `smsa` on the predicted wage. Then use `margins, at(wedyrs=(5(1)17))` to compute predicted wages across the observed range of wife's education years, holding other variables at their means. Describe the shape of the relationship.

5. *(Bonus)* **Interaction term** — Re-run the regression from Task 3 but add an interaction between `wedyrs` and `smsa` (i.e., `c.wedyrs#i.smsa`). Use `margins, dydx(wedyrs) over(smsa)` to estimate whether the return to education differs between SMSA and non-SMSA women. Is the difference economically meaningful?

- **Hint 1 (Task 1)**: The two-sample t-test syntax in Stata is `ttest varname, by(groupvar)` — look at the row labeled "diff" in the output to find your t-statistic and two-sided p-value.
- **Hint 2 (Task 3)**: You can include multiple regressors simply by listing them after the dependent variable: `regress y x1 x2 x3 x4`. Think about what sign you'd expect on `kl6` if young children reduce labor market attachment and employer-perceived commitment.
- **Hint 3 (Task 4)**: After `regress`, `margins` with no options gives fitted values at the mean of all covariates; `margins, dydx(smsa)` gives the marginal effect of the binary SMSA indicator. For the `at()` syntax, check `help margins` for how to specify a numlist with `(min(step)max)`.
- **Hint 4 (Bonus)**: When you include an interaction with `c.wedyrs#i.smsa`, Stata's `margins` command can decompose the effect of `wedyrs` separately for each value of `smsa` — no manual calculation needed.
