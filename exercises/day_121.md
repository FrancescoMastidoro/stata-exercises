## Background
The `laborsup` dataset contains 500 observations on female labor supply, including women's earnings, education, husbands' education, number of children, and other household income. Instrumental variables (IV) estimation is essential in labor economics when a regressor like education or income is correlated with unobserved factors that also affect the outcome, and finding a valid instrument allows researchers to recover causal effects.

## Your tasks

1. **Explore the data** — Load the dataset and run `describe` and `summarize`. Note the ranges and types of all six variables. Then run a simple OLS regression of `fem_inc` on `fem_educ`, `kids`, and `other_inc`. Record the coefficient on `fem_educ`.

2. **Check instrument relevance** — Regress `fem_educ` on the proposed instrument `male_educ`, controlling for `kids` and `other_inc`. Report the coefficient on `male_educ` and its t-statistic. Is the instrument correlated with the endogenous variable?

3. **Run 2SLS by hand** — Using the first-stage regression from Task 2, generate the fitted values of `fem_educ` (call them `fem_educ_hat`). Then regress `fem_inc` on `fem_educ_hat`, `kids`, and `other_inc`. Compare the coefficient on the fitted values to your OLS estimate from Task 1.

4. **Run 2SLS using `ivregress`** — Use `ivregress 2sls` to regress `fem_inc` on `kids` and `other_inc`, instrumenting `fem_educ` with `male_educ`. After the regression, run `estat firststage` to obtain the first-stage F-statistic and comment on whether the instrument is strong (conventional threshold: F > 10).

5. *(Bonus)* **Test for endogeneity** — After your `ivregress` regression, use `estat endogenous` to formally test whether `fem_educ` is endogenous. State the null hypothesis of the test, report the p-value, and explain what the result implies for whether OLS or IV is preferred in this context.
