## Background
The Grunfeld dataset contains annual investment, market value, and capital stock data for 10 large U.S. firms from 1935 to 1954, making it a classic panel used in econometrics teaching. Difference-in-Differences (DiD) is a cornerstone method in labor and applied economics for estimating causal effects when treatment is assigned at the group level and you observe units both before and after an intervention.

## Your tasks

1. **Explore the panel structure** — Load the dataset, declare it as a panel with `xtset`, and use `xtdescribe` to confirm you have a balanced panel. Report how many companies there are and how many time periods.

2. **Create a treatment indicator** — Suppose a fictional policy was introduced in **1945** that affected only companies 1 through 5 (the "treated" group). Create a binary variable `treated` equal to 1 for companies 1–5 and 0 otherwise. Then create a `post` variable equal to 1 for years 1945 and later, and 0 otherwise. Finally, create the interaction term `did = treated * post`.

3. **Run the basic DiD regression** — Regress `invest` on `treated`, `post`, and `did`. Interpret the coefficient on `did`: what does it represent in the DiD framework? Does investment appear to change differentially for treated firms after 1945?

4. **Add company and year fixed effects** — Re-estimate the DiD model using `xtreg` with fixed effects (the `fe` option) and year dummies (`i.year`). Compare the coefficient on `did` before and after adding fixed effects. Use `areg` with `absorb(company)` and `i.year` as an alternative approach and confirm you get the same point estimate.

5. *(Bonus)* **Parallel trends check** — Generate a variable `year_group` that divides the pre-treatment period (1935–1944) into two sub-periods: `year_group = 1` for 1935–1939 and `year_group = 2` for 1940–1944. Restrict the sample to pre-treatment years only, then regress `invest` on `treated`, `i.year_group`, and their interaction. If the parallel trends assumption holds, what should you expect the interaction coefficient to look like, and why?
