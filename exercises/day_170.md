## Background
The `laborsup` dataset contains 500 observations on female labor supply, including wife's income, husband's education and income, and number of children. Instrumental variables (IV) estimation is essential in labor economics whenever a regressor is endogenous — for example, when a woman's income and her labor force participation are jointly determined.

## Your tasks

1. **Explore the data** — Load the dataset and run `describe` and `summarize`. Note which variables are binary (`fem_work`) versus continuous (`fem_inc`, `other_inc`, `fem_educ`, `male_educ`, `kids`). Report the mean and standard deviation of `fem_inc` and `other_inc`.

2. **OLS baseline** — Regress `fem_inc` on `kids`, `fem_educ`, and `other_inc`. Store the results using `estimates store ols`. Interpret the sign and significance of the coefficient on `kids`.

3. **First-stage check** — To use `male_educ` as an instrument for `other_inc`, you must verify relevance. Regress `other_inc` on `male_educ`, `kids`, and `fem_educ`. Report the F-statistic and comment on whether `male_educ` is a strong instrument (rule of thumb: F > 10).

4. **IV estimation** — Use `ivregress 2sls` to regress `fem_inc` on `kids` and `fem_educ`, instrumenting `other_inc` with `male_educ`. Store the results as `estimates store iv`. Then use `estimates table ols iv, b se` to compare OLS and IV coefficients side by side — does endogeneity appear to matter?

5. *(Bonus)* **Hausman test** — After running both OLS and IV, use `hausman iv ols` to formally test whether `other_inc` is endogenous. Interpret the chi-squared statistic and its p-value: do you reject exogeneity at the 5% level? Then re-run `ivregress` adding the `, robust` option and explain why robust standard errors might be preferred here even if the Hausman test result does not change.
