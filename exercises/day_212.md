## Background
The `laborsup` dataset contains information on 500 women, including their own education, their partner's education, number of children, income variables, and labor force participation. Instrumental variables (IV) estimation is a cornerstone technique in labor economics when OLS suffers from endogeneity — for example, when a regressor like `fem_educ` may be correlated with unobserved ability or family background that also affects earnings.

## Your tasks

1. **Explore the data** — Load the dataset and run `describe` and `summarize`. Note the ranges of `fem_educ`, `male_educ`, `fem_inc`, `other_inc`, and `kids`. Which variables look like plausible instruments for female education?

2. **OLS benchmark** — Regress `fem_inc` on `fem_educ`, `kids`, and `other_inc`. Interpret the coefficient on `fem_educ` and record the R-squared. This is your baseline to compare against IV.

3. **First-stage regression** — Regress `fem_educ` on `male_educ`, `kids`, and `other_inc`. Check whether `male_educ` is a strong predictor (look at its t-statistic and the overall F-statistic). A weak instrument is one where the F-statistic on the excluded instrument is below 10 — does yours pass?

4. **IV estimation with `ivregress`** — Use `ivregress 2sls fem_inc (fem_educ = male_educ) kids other_inc`. Compare the IV coefficient on `fem_educ` to your OLS estimate from Task 2. Then run `estat firststage` to retrieve the first-stage F-statistic formally. Does the direction or magnitude of the coefficient change substantially, and what might that tell you about endogeneity bias in OLS?

5. *(Bonus)* **Hausman-style endogeneity test** — After your `ivregress` estimation, run `estat endogenous` to test whether `fem_educ` is statistically endogenous. Report the test statistic and p-value, and write a brief comment in your `.do` file interpreting whether OLS or IV is preferred based on this result. As an additional challenge, try `ivregress liml` as an alternative estimator and compare its coefficient on `fem_educ` to the 2SLS result.
